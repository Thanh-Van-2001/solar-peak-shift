Utility Solar Is Moving the Net-Load Peak: Evidence from 42 U.S. Balancing Authorities, 2019–2026

Utility-scale solar pushes the daily net-load peak (demand minus solar and wind) into the evening, when storage and dispatchable capacity must ramp up. Evidence comes mostly from single systems or simulations; a multi-market assessment through 2020 found solar's net-load effects clear only in California and the Southwest. With hourly EIA-930 data for 42 U.S. balancing authorities (BAs) and a pre-specified plan, we compare 2019–2020 and 2025–2026 summers in high- and low-solar BAs and score the operators' demand forecasts. In the 18 BAs where utility solar supplied at least 5% of 2025 demand, the median hour of the daily net-load peak moved later in all 17 BAs with a change (Holm p < 0.001), by a median of 1 hour (mean 1.6). The share of days peaking at hour ending 20 or later rose from 8% to 65%, the gap to the demand peak widened in 13 of 14 BAs, and the shift exceeded that of low-solar BAs (Holm p = 0.001). Exploratory checks find a continuous peak time later in all 18 BAs (median 1.4 hours), close to the shift predicted by scaling 2019–2020 solar profiles to 2025–2026 volumes, and attribute it to solar, not wind. The three-hour ramp into the peak grew a median 1.5-fold, and on a median 71% of days the peak came two or more hours after the demand peak, which demand-peak programmes may miss. The operators' demand forecasts were not measurably less accurate at the net-load peak than at the demand peak.

Keywords: Solar energy, net load, peak demand, load forecasting, EIA-930.
