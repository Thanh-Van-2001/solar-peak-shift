Utility Solar Is Moving the Net-Load Peak: Evidence from 42 U.S. Balancing Authorities, 2019–2026

Utility-scale solar pushes the daily peak of net load (demand minus solar and wind) into the evening, when storage and dispatchable capacity must ramp up. Evidence comes mostly from single systems, chiefly California, or simulations; a multi-market assessment through 2020 found solar's net-load effects clear only in California and the Southwest. With hourly EIA-930 data for 42 U.S. balancing authorities (BAs) and a pre-specified plan, we compare the summers of 2019–2020 and 2025–2026 in high- and low-solar BAs, separate solar from wind, and score the operators' demand forecasts. In the 18 BAs where utility solar supplied at least 5% of 2025 demand, the median hour of the daily net-load peak moved later in all 17 BAs with a change (Holm p < 0.001), by a median of 1 hour per BA (mean 1.6). The share of days with the net-load peak at hour ending 20 or later rose from 8% to 65%, the gap to the demand peak widened in 13 of 14 BAs, and the shift exceeded that of low-solar BAs (Holm p = 0.001). Exploratory checks attribute the shift to solar, not wind; it survives month stratification, 2019 against 2025, and excluding BAs with suspect solar timestamps. The median three-hour ramp into the net-load peak grew 1.5-fold per BA. The operators' demand forecast was not measurably less accurate at the net-load peak hour than at the demand peak hour. Peak-oriented tariffs, demand response and forecast evaluation in solar-rich BAs should follow the net-load peak.

Keywords: Solar energy, net load, peak demand, load forecasting, EIA-930.
